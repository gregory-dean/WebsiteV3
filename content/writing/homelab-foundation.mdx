## Why a homelab

I wanted a controlled place to practice both sides of the fight: administer systems like a defender, then attack them like an adversary, without putting anyone else's network at risk. The [Homelab repo](https://github.com/gregory-dean/Homelab) tracks the build in public so the process itself becomes a portfolio artifact.

## Architecture

The lab simulates a small enterprise environment on a Windows 11 host with Oracle VirtualBox:

- Internal subnet `192.168.56.0/24` on a host-only adapter, with NAT per VM for controlled outbound access
- **DC-01**, a Windows Server 2022 domain controller providing Active Directory and internal DNS
- **WINCLIENT-01**, a domain-joined workstation for authentication and Group Policy validation
- **KALI-01**, an off-domain attack machine for offensive testing
- **UBUNTU-01**, a Linux server for services and log generation
- **SIEM-01**, a Wazuh platform for log aggregation, detection, and alerting

## The build, phase by phase

1. **Lab foundation and networking**, complete
2. **AD identity and access**, complete
3. **Log collection and SIEM onboarding**, in progress
4. **Vulnerability management workflow**, planned
5. **Attack simulation and detection**, planned
6. **Linux hardening and monitoring**, planned

The SIEM phase is where the lab starts paying off. I'm deploying Wazuh agents alongside Sysmon to get real endpoint visibility into process creation, PowerShell activity, and lateral movement indicators, then tuning detection rules and alert thresholds so coverage stays high while false positives drop.

## Tooling in play

Wazuh, Sysmon, Nmap, Wireshark, Nessus and OpenVAS, Metasploit, and Burp Suite sit alongside Active Directory, DNS, and Group Policy. The goal isn't collecting logos. It's generating realistic telemetry and practicing the full loop from exploit to detection to remediation.

## What's next

Once the logs are trustworthy, attack simulation becomes useful instead of noisy. Each phase gets its own writeup in the repo, including asset inventories, architecture docs, and the troubleshooting notes from everything that broke along the way. Those notes might be the most valuable part.
